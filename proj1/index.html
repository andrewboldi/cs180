Prologue · 1907

Three grayscale plates filtered by RGB

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in color before color film existed. His thought process was to take a picture of each scene three times through a blue, a green, and a red filter. The Library of Congress digitized the grayscale images — blue on top, then green, then red.

The task

Split the image into three parts, treat the blue plate as the anchor, and recover two displacement vectors for the red and regreen images such that they eventually align with each other. The plates are slightly shifted between exposures (e.g. from the camera or subject moving), so every image needs its own pair of offsets.

Part 01 · Single-scale search

Exhaustive search

For the small JPEG plates, we can manually search across a specific grid for alignment of a pair of plates and score each candidate by a distance function.

L2 / Euclidean distance

d(A,B) = √ Σᵢⱼ ( Aᵢⱼ − Bᵢⱼ )²

Sum of squared pixel differences where lower is better. This is a fast and simple metric, but it assumes the two channels have similar brightness, which glass plates shot through different filters often do not.

Normalized cross-correlation

ncc(A,B) = ⟨ (A−Ā)∕‖A−Ā‖ , (B−B̄)∕‖B−B̄‖ ⟩

The NCC computes the dot product of both channels after subtracting their means and normalizing. This is kind of like a cosine similarity and higher scores are better. This scoring method is also invariant to affine brightness changes between plates.

Implementation

One issue with blindly applying the above scoring functions to a pair of plates is the algorithm will tend to score highly alignments where the non-image borders align the best. For example, the algorithm would align the white or black border margins the best. To counteract for this, we can compute the scoring on interior pixels only. For example, we can set a 10% border margin so plate borders are excluded from the scoring. One performance optimization we can make during the scoring function is to use einsum to vectorize the operations. Eventually, we obtain offsets (written as (x, y)) where positive x moves a plate right and positive y moves it down. Both metrics converged on identical offsets for all three small plates.

Part 02 · Coarse to fine

The pyramid

Now, the above images were a reduced scale .JPG file which makes searching over a 30px by 30px window tractable. However, as we scale the resolution to ~3700 × 3200 pixels as in the original images, exhaustive search at that size would require a bigger search window and therefore increase the number of alignment trials. To solve for this, we can use a clever trick where we iteratively solve for the precise alignment by starting at a scaled down image and working our way up to the original resolution.

How it works

We manually check if a plate is wider than 1024 pixels and if not, recursively align the images. Whatever offset comes back from lower calls is doubled to account for the halving of the resolution. At the base of the recursion (smallest image) we run a generous grid search of 100px x 100px. However, as we work our way up to higher resolutions, since we believe we've reached the general location of the global optimum, we can reduce the grid search to 10px x 10px and simply refine the search. This optimization brings down the full search optimization from several minutes to about 30 seconds per image.

Observations

Images shown are the NCC results. L2 and NCC got exactly the same results for all the images. The full table of every offset, both metrics, is in the appendix below. Some interesting artifacts/failures we observe can generally be attributed to motion in the images. For example, on harvesters the red plate carries a few pixels of residual vertical fringe show up under both metrics. Since most of the subjects in the image are perfectly aligned with only a couple outliers, it's reasonable to assume that those subjects moved between exposures.
