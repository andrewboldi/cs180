Prologue · 1907

Three plates, one scene

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in color before color film existed: every scene was exposed three times onto a glass plate, through a blue, a green, and a red filter. The Library of Congress digitized the surviving negatives as tall strips — blue on top, then green, then red.

The task

Cut the strip into thirds, treat the blue plate as the anchor, and recover two displacement vectors — one that seats green on blue, one that seats red on blue. The plates shifted between exposures (the camera moved, the subject breathed), so every image needs its own pair of offsets, found automatically.

Part 01 · Single-scale search

Exhaustive, but honest

For the small JPEG plates, brute force is enough: slide the green and red channels over blue through every offset in a ±15-pixel window, score each candidate, keep the best one.

L2 / Euclidean distance

d(A,B) = √ Σᵢⱼ ( Aᵢⱼ − Bᵢⱼ )²

Sum of squared pixel differences — lower is better. Fast and simple, but it assumes the two channels have similar brightness, which glass plates shot through different filters often do not.

Normalized cross-correlation

ncc(A,B) = ⟨ (A−Ā)∕‖A−Ā‖ , (B−B̄)∕‖B−B̄‖ ⟩

The dot product of both channels after subtracting their means and normalizing — a cosine similarity, higher is better. Invariant to affine brightness changes between plates.

Implementation

Scores are computed on interior pixels only — a 50-pixel margin is ignored so the plate borders can't vote. Rather than looping over the 31 × 31 = 961 candidate offsets one at a time, all shifted copies are stacked into a single array and scored in one vectorized einsum; the whole search runs in a blink. Offsets below are written (x, y): positive x moves a plate right, positive y moves it down. Both metrics chose identical offsets on all three small plates.

Naive Aligned · NCC

Observations

Monastery's green plate actually needs to move up (negative y) — a reminder that the exposures drifted in both directions. With shifts this small, L2 and NCC agree exactly; the differences between the metrics only start to matter when the plates disagree about brightness, which is coming in Part 03.

Part 02 · Coarse to fine

The pyramid

The full scans are ~3700 × 3200 pixels with displacements beyond a hundred pixels — exhaustive search at that size would evaluate thousands of 12-megapixel differences. The pyramid finds the big moves where pixels are cheap.

How it works

If a plate is wider than 1024 pixels, halve it (area-averaged downsample) and align the halves recursively. Whatever offset comes back is doubled — one pixel at half resolution is two at full — and then refined with a small ±5 search at the current scale. The base of the recursion, where the image is small, runs a generous ±50 search. Every level pays for only a constant-size window, so a 128-pixel displacement is discovered near the top of the pyramid for pennies and merely polished on the way down. Scoring always ignores a 10% border so the plate edges and frame damage can't dominate either metric.

Observations

Images shown are the NCC results; L2 landed within a pixel of NCC on every provided plate, so the two sets are visually identical. The full table of every offset, both metrics, is in the appendix below. On harvesters the red plate carries a few pixels of residual vertical fringe under both metrics — the crowd moved between exposures, so no single translation can be perfectly right everywhere.

Part 03 · Failure analysis

Where raw pixels lie

One plate defeats brightness-based matching: the Emir of Bukhara. His robe is brilliant blue silk — blazing in the blue plate, nearly black in the red plate. The two channels genuinely disagree about what is bright.

Naive Pyramid · NCC

What went wrong

The pyramid gets the green plate right — G (+24, +49) · R (+56, +104) for NCC — but the red plate lands at R (+56, +104), roughly fifteen pixels right of where it belongs. As an independent reference I phase-correlated the raw red plate directly against the blue one, which puts the true displacement near R (+41, +106). Both L2 and NCC choose nearly the same wrong basin: at the pyramid's coarsest level, the inverted brightness of the robe outweighs the scene's actual structure, and every finer level then faithfully polishes a minimum that was wrong to begin with. This is the one misalignment in the set — and the classic argument for matching on gradients or edges instead of raw intensities, where the robe's brightness flip stops mattering.

Part 04 · From the archive

Five more plates

Five additional negatives chosen from the Library of Congress collection and run through the same pyramid, untouched parameters: rivers, gardens, and a railway bridge. Both metrics agreed within a pixel on every one.
